How the Sky Friction Index works

What goes into it, how it was tested against real FAA records, and where it falls short.

What the number means

The Sky Friction Index is the estimated chance that 3 or more new FAA ground stops or ground delay programs start at US airports in the next 3 hours. We call that a "bad stretch". It happened in about 20% of hours in the test period.

Levels: below 10% low · 10–25% moderate · 25–50% elevated · 50% or more high. The dashboard also shows what is typical for the same hour, so you can see whether today is unusual.

The three are combined with a logistic model whose weights were fitted to the FAA records, not chosen by hand. The hubs are JFK, LGA, EWR, BOS, PHL, ATL, CLT, MIA, ORD, DFW, IAH, DEN, SFO, SEA and LAX.

The 1–10 score on the dashboard

To make the outlook easy to read, each input is shown as a score from 1 to 10 where 5 is average: the input's rank among all past hours, times ten. A 2 means calmer than about 80% of hours; an 8 means busier than about 80%.

The final score is the weighted average of the three: time of day 50%, recent FAA programs 35%, weather at US hubs 15%, rounded to a whole number. Example: 0.50 × 2 + 0.35 × 2 + 0.15 × 7 = 2.8 → 3.

It is slightly less precise in exchange for being easy to read and check by hand. In this simple form, equal weights work about as well; the 50/35/15 split comes from the tested model.

The formula and the weights

In plain terms: each input is compared with its average, scaled by how much it varies, multiplied by its weight, and added up. The total is turned into a chance between 0% and 100% with the standard logistic curve.

chance = 1 / (1 + e−z), where z = intercept + Σ weighti × (inputi − averagei) / spreadi

How it was tested

  • Yardstick: every US ground stop and ground delay program in the FAA's public advisory archive from mid-July to mid-November 2026: 2,788 programs.
  • Honest split: the model was fitted on the first two-thirds of the period and scored only on the last third (six weeks), which it never saw.
  • Score: how often an hour that was followed by a bad stretch is ranked above one that wasn't. 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect. (Statisticians call this AUC.)
  • Rivals: the index has to beat simpler alternatives, including time of day alone.

Known weakness: the season. The model learned mostly from the summer thunderstorm season, so on the calmer autumn test weeks its percentages ran too high, even though its ranking of risky versus calm hours held up. To correct for this, the live model is re-centred on the most recent 30 days so its average matches what is actually happening now. It will be refitted monthly as GAPS's own stored history grows.

Per-airport outlooks

For each of 15 major US hubs, GAPS also estimates the chance that the FAA starts a new ground stop or ground delay program at that airport in the next 3 hours. One model covers all 15 airports, so each weight is learned from far more examples than any single airport could provide. Its inputs:

  • Weather at the airport: the same bad-weather points as the index, worst hour of the next 3, at that airport only.
  • Programs at the airport in the last ~3 hours and the last 24 hours.
  • Programs at other airports in the last ~3 hours: a sign the whole system is under strain.
  • The airport's usual rate at this hour on a weekday or weekend.

Tested the same way as the index: fitted on the first two-thirds of the period and scored on the held-out weeks. A program already in effect makes a new one less likely, so an airport under a long delay program can show a low chance.

Does "20%" mean 20%?

Same seasonal weakness as the index. On the autumn test weeks the higher estimates ran too high (the model learned mostly from summer storms). The live model is re-centred on the most recent 30 days. Airports with very few programs in the test weeks, such as LAX, cannot be judged on their own; their outlooks lean on the pooled model.

Limits

  • US only. The FAA publishes free disruption records; Europe's EUROCONTROL data needs an organisation agreement.
  • Not individual flights. It estimates network-level disruption, not whether your flight will be late.
  • Traffic is not in the index. No free archive of past aircraft counts exists, so traffic could not be tested. The dashboard shows it as context.
  • Test weather is model data. The archive of airport weather reports (METARs) throttled our requests, so the test used Open-Meteo's archived forecasts. The live index uses the same Open-Meteo forecasts, so the two match.
  • Four months of data. That includes one summer storm season and part of autumn. Winter (snow, de-icing) is not represented yet.

Reproduce it

All readings and the FAA records are downloadable on the open data page; the model card summarises the models, results and change history.

The scripts are in the repository under scripts/backtest/: they download the FAA archive and historical weather, fit the model and write the results shown here (results · model).
